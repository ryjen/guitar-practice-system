# Country Picking Technique Branch

## Goal

Provide an independently selectable country-guitar branch that extends existing rock rhythm, lead, muting, bending, and pentatonic skills without making country practice mandatory. The initial path emphasizes country-rock and Bakersfield-adjacent electric playing before more specialized session vocabulary.

## Selection boundary

This branch is optional. Activate it for a focused block, song use case, or backing-track project, then return to the primary slide, wah, or E-Bow path without losing state.

Recommended cadence:

- exploration: one short session per week
- active branch: two sessions per week for four to eight weeks
- maintenance: one contextual take per month

## Prerequisites

Good-enough prerequisites:

- steady eighth-note rhythm at moderate tempo
- basic alternate picking and palm muting
- major and minor pentatonic familiarity
- controlled whole- and half-step bends
- basic I-IV-V recognition

Helpful but not required:

- top-three-string triads
- fingerstyle experience
- chord-tone targeting
- clean-tone muting discipline

## Default setup

- clean or edge-of-breakup tone
- bridge or bridge/middle pickup where available
- moderate treble; avoid using brightness to simulate articulation
- light compression only after dynamics and noise are audible dry
- short slapback or room ambience only in musical-context work

Compression can improve consistency and sustain, but it also raises pick noise and can flatten intentional accents. Technique gates should pass with compression bypassed or used lightly.

## Baseline recording

Record one uninterrupted take containing:

1. Eight bars of alternating-bass I-IV-V rhythm.
2. Four bars of pick-and-finger string alternation.
3. Four double-stop phrases.
4. One short major/minor pentatonic line.
5. One pedal-steel-style bend phrase.
6. One chorus over a clean country-rock backing track.

Note the dominant defect: timing, string separation, excess noise, weak snap, uncontrolled volume, bend intonation, chord-tone awareness, or tension.

## Progression

### Phase 1 — pick-and-finger independence

Use the pick for lower strings and middle/ring fingers for higher strings.

Practice:

- alternate pick on string 5 with middle finger on string 2
- repeat with strings 6/3 and 4/1
- play simultaneous pinch, then separated low-high attacks
- keep unused strings muted

Gate:

- four bars remain even at 70-90 BPM
- pick and finger attacks are distinct but balanced
- unused strings never dominate

### Phase 2 — alternating bass and boom-chuck

Practice root/fifth bass movement with short upper-string chord attacks.

Progression:

1. I chord only.
2. I-IV.
3. I-IV-V with two-bar changes.
4. Full eight- or twelve-bar form.

Gate:

- bass notes land consistently on intended beats
- chord attacks are short and do not blur the bass line
- transitions do not interrupt pulse

### Phase 3 — hybrid-picked double-stops

Focus on thirds, sixths, and adjacent-string dyads.

Practice:

- pick lower note and pluck upper note simultaneously
- alternate simultaneous and staggered attacks
- move one voice while holding the other
- resolve dyads into chord tones

Gate:

- both notes speak clearly without accidental adjacent strings
- volume balance is intentional
- resolutions agree with the harmony

### Phase 4 — open-string articulation

Add open-string pull-offs, hammer-ons, and cross-string figures.

Practice:

- fretted note to open-string pull-off
- picked bass note plus upper open-string response
- short three-note patterns crossing one open string
- transpose only where the open string remains harmonically useful

Gate:

- open strings are intentional, in time, and correctly damped
- pull-offs do not become louder than picked notes by accident
- phrases stop cleanly at chord changes

### Phase 5 — chord-tone targeting and triads

Map major triads and dominant-seventh fragments on the top three strings.

Practice:

- identify nearest root, third, and fifth for I, IV, and V
- play one two-beat fill into each chord change
- restrict phrases to one triad shape before connecting positions
- use chromatic approach notes only when the target is clear

Gate:

- strong-beat arrivals reflect the current chord
- fills leave space for the imagined vocal
- triad movement is visible without relying on a scale box

### Phase 6 — major/minor pentatonic blending

Treat the tonal contrast as controlled vocabulary, not random note mixing.

Practice:

- one phrase using only major pentatonic
- one using only minor pentatonic
- call-and-response between the two
- resolve minor-third colour to the major third where appropriate

Gate:

- the listener can hear deliberate major/minor contrast
- altered colour notes resolve convincingly
- lines remain rhythmically shaped rather than scale-like

### Phase 7 — chicken-picking articulation

Develop short, muted, percussive notes and controlled finger snap.

Practice:

- muted pick/finger alternation on one chord
- fretted note followed by dead-note response
- short snapped dyads with immediate release
- accent displacement across eighth- and sixteenth-note grids

Gate:

- snap is produced without excessive finger force
- dead notes and pitched notes remain distinguishable
- four bars stay relaxed and aligned to the groove

### Phase 8 — pedal-steel-style bends

Use oblique motion: hold one note while bending another into a consonant interval.

Practice:

- hold upper note while bending lower note a half or whole step
- reverse the voice roles where practical
- pre-bend and release into a chord tone
- use volume swell only after bend pitch is reliable

Gate:

- bent note reaches pitch against the stationary reference
- stationary note remains stable
- release is controlled and rhythmically placed

### Phase 9 — musical phrase construction

Combine alternating bass, fills, double-stops, open strings, bends, and rests in a complete form.

Requirements:

- one rhythm section with no fills
- one fill between phrases
- one double-stop response
- one bend phrase
- at least one full bar of space

Gate:

- technique choices serve the arrangement
- rhythm remains stable when moving between accompaniment and lead
- the take does not become continuous fill playing

## Backing-track scenarios

### Clean I-IV-V practice track

Planned asset ID: `country-i-iv-v`.

Specification:

- 4/4 at 80-100 BPM
- clear two-beat or one-bar bass motion
- restrained drums with audible backbeat
- sparse piano or acoustic rhythm
- eight- or twelve-bar loop
- separate drums, bass, and keys/acoustic MIDI tracks

Use for alternating bass, chord-tone fills, double-stops, and major/minor blending.

### Country-rock full-form track

Planned asset ID: `country-rock-form`.

Specification:

- 4/4 at 96-120 BPM
- intro, verse, chorus, and turnaround markers
- stronger rock drums and bass
- explicit gaps for fills
- no competing lead guitar

Use for switching between rhythm and lead roles, recovery after mistakes, and arrangement discipline.

### Slow bend and double-stop bed

A sparse 60-76 BPM I-IV or I-IV-V bed can share arrangement infrastructure with slide practice while using a cleaner, less blues-dominant harmonic treatment. Reuse assets where their musical function genuinely overlaps; do not duplicate only to rename the genre.

## Song and passage use cases

Songs remain use cases rather than the curriculum. Select legally sourced or short self-transcribed passages that expose:

- alternating bass under chord changes
- hybrid-picked double-stops
- open-string pull-off phrases
- major/minor pentatonic contrast
- pedal-steel-style bends
- fills between vocal lines
- clean country-rock rhythm

For each passage, record section/time range, techniques exercised, key, tuning, tempo, prerequisite, and licensed notation source when applicable.

## Practice sessions

### 15-minute branch check

- 3 min pick/finger independence
- 4 min alternating bass
- 4 min current phrase technique
- 4 min recorded I-IV-V take

### 30-minute development

- 5 min clean muting and balance
- 8 min current phase drill
- 7 min triad/chord-tone work
- 8 min backing-track take
- 2 min evidence note

### 45-minute musical session

- 5 min diagnostic
- 10 min rhythm role
- 10 min lead articulation
- 15 min two uninterrupted full-form takes
- 5 min review of timing, space, and harmonic targeting

## Quality gates

| Dimension | Gate |
|---|---|
| Timing | Bass, chord attacks, fills, and pedal notes stay aligned through a complete form. |
| String separation | Intended notes speak clearly; open and adjacent strings are controlled. |
| Dynamics | Pick and finger attacks are balanced, with deliberate accents and snap. |
| Muting | Dead notes, short notes, and sustained notes are clearly differentiated. |
| Intonation | Pedal-steel bends reach pitch against a stable reference note. |
| Harmony | Strong-beat targets reflect the underlying chord. |
| Independence | Switching between rhythm and fills does not break the groove. |
| Repeatability | Three isolated passes succeed in two sessions. |
| Musical context | Two uninterrupted takes demonstrate rhythm and lead roles with intentional space. |

## Maintenance

- Active branch: every one to two weeks.
- Inactive but retained: monthly or when a song requires it.
- Before recording: one alternating-bass form, one double-stop phrase, and one bend-intontation check.

Regression check:

1. Four bars pick/finger alternation.
2. One I-IV-V alternating-bass form.
3. Two double-stop resolutions.
4. One major/minor contrast phrase.
5. One pedal-steel bend against a reference note.

Regress only the failed dimension where practical.

## Completion boundary

The documented branch is complete when prerequisites, independent activation rules, staged progression, backing scenarios, setup trade-offs, song-use-case rules, quality gates, and maintenance are defined.

Personal completion still requires baseline and musical-context recordings.
